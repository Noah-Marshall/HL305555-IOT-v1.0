2.0 Learning Outcomes

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

  • Explain the purpose of a Health Technology Assessment and the decision it helps inform.
  • Distinguish rapid relative effectiveness assessment from comprehensive HTA.
  • Identify the nine EUnetHTA domains and describe what each examines.

2.1 What Is a Health Technology Assessment?

A Health Technology Assessment (HTA) evaluates a health technology to inform decisions about its use. Rather than considering effectiveness alone, an HTA examines the technology's benefits, harms, costs and wider implications for people, services and society.

The overarching question to keep in mind throughout your assessment is: Should this technology be used for this health problem and population, and, if so, under what conditions?

You do not need to state this question explicitly in your report. Instead, use it to guide which evidence you include, how you evaluate the different domains and the recommendation you reach.

Health technology assessments can take different forms and use different methodological frameworks, depending on their purpose and scope. For this textbook, we will use the EUnetHTA HTA Core Model, version 3.0 (2016), which provides a structured approach to assessing health technologies across nine domains. You can consult the full HTA Core Model 3.0 (PDF) for further detail.

2.2 What Are the HTA Domains?

The EUnetHTA HTA Core Model provides nine domains for examining a technology from different perspectives. A rapid relative effectiveness assessment focuses on health problem and current use, technical characteristics, safety and clinical effectiveness. A comprehensive HTA additionally examines economics, ethics, organisation, patient and social aspects, and law.

The table below summarises what each domain covers.

HTA domainWhat it examines
Health Problem and Current Use (CUR)Defines the health problem, the people affected, its burden, and how the condition is currently managed. It also considers existing use of the technology.
Technical Characteristics (TEC)Explains what the technology is, how it works, its main features, intended users and practical requirements.
Clinical Effectiveness (EFF)Examines whether the technology improves relevant health outcomes, how large and lasting any benefits are, and how it compares with alternatives.
Safety (SAF)Examines potential harms, unwanted effects and safety concerns, including whether particular groups may face greater risks.
Costs and Economic Effectiveness (ECO)Considers the resources needed to provide the technology, its costs and whether its benefits represent value for money compared with alternatives.
Ethical Analysis (ETH)Considers ethical questions such as autonomy, privacy, fairness and the balance between potential benefits and harms.
Organisational Aspects (ORG)Explores how introducing the technology would affect services, staff, training, workflows and delivery.
Patient and Social Aspects (SOC)Considers the experiences, preferences and needs of patients and users, including acceptability, access and effects on different social groups.
Legal Aspects (LEG)Identifies laws, regulations and legal responsibilities relevant to providing and using the technology, such as data protection and medical-device requirements.

3.0 Learning Outcomes

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

  • Write in a professional tone without conversational expressions, slang, contractions or overly personal language.
  • Use scientific language that accurately reflects evidence and supports clear conclusions.
  • Make writing concise without losing essential information.
  • Explain technical jargon clearly and use geographical, population and outcome terms consistently.
  • Construct clear sentences, coherent paragraphs and purposeful report sections.

3.1 Professional Language

Academic writing should use a professional, formal and accessible tone. Avoid the following:

  • Conversational expressions: Avoid phrases commonly used in everyday speech. For example, write “however” rather than “having said that”, or “consider” rather than “have a look at”.
  • Slang: Avoid informal or colloquial words and expressions. For example, write “numerous studies” rather than “loads of studies”, or “beneficial” rather than “really great”.
  • Contractions: Write words in full rather than using shortened forms. For example, write “cannot” rather than “can't”, “do not” rather than “don't”, and “it is” rather than “it's”.
  • Overly personal language: Avoid unnecessary references to yourself or the reader. For example, write “This report evaluates the effectiveness of wearable fitness trackers” rather than “I am going to look at whether fitness trackers work”.

Professional language should remain clear and natural. The aim is not to make writing unnecessarily complicated, but to communicate ideas accurately and appropriately.

Less appropriateImproved
“We can't tell if these trackers are any good because loads of studies don't look at long-term use.”“The long-term effectiveness of activity trackers remains uncertain because many studies have limited follow-up periods.”

Why is this better? The revised sentence removes contractions, conversational phrasing and an informal judgement while retaining the intended meaning.

Your Turn

Rewrite the following sentence using professional academic language. Remove contractions, slang, conversational phrasing and unnecessary personal language.

Example to improve: “I think fitness trackers are really great, but we can’t tell if they’re actually any good for students because loads of studies don’t look at what happens later.”

Suggested improved version

“Evidence on the long-term benefits of activity trackers for university students remains limited because many studies have short follow-up periods.”

3.2 Scientific Language

Scientific language should be precise, objective and evidence-based. The words you choose should reflect the findings and limitations of the evidence.

  • Use appropriately qualified language: Choose words that reflect what the evidence demonstrates. For example, a study may “find”, “report”, “suggest” or “indicate” an effect, but rarely “proves” that a technology works in every situation.
  • Distinguish association from causation: Two variables being associated does not necessarily mean that one causes the other. For example, “fitness tracker use was associated with increased physical activity” does not establish that using a tracker caused the increase.
  • Acknowledge uncertainty: Distinguish preliminary findings from established evidence. For example, write “early findings suggest that the technology may increase physical activity” rather than “the technology is an effective solution” when the available evidence is limited.
  • Use precise terminology: Describe what was actually measured rather than making broader claims. For example, write “participants reported increased weekly physical activity” rather than “participants experienced better health” if physical activity was the only outcome measured.
  • Draw clear, evidence-informed conclusions: Interpret individual studies cautiously, but communicate a clear overall judgement when the combined evidence supports one. For example, rather than “fitness trackers might possibly be useful for some people”, write “fitness trackers are recommended as a supplementary tool for increasing physical activity, provided appropriate support is available”—but only if the evidence justifies that recommendation.
Less appropriateImproved
“This study proves that activity trackers make all students more active.”“The study reported increased physical activity among participating students, although the findings may not generalise to all university students.”

Why is this better? The revision states the measured finding without making an unsupported causal or universal claim, and identifies an important limit to its interpretation.

Your Turn

Rewrite the following claim using precise, appropriately qualified scientific language. Avoid unsupported conclusions about causation and how widely the result applies.

Example to improve: “This study proves that AI mental health chatbots cure depression in all university students because participants using the chatbot reported fewer symptoms.”

Suggested improved version

“The study found that chatbot users reported fewer depressive symptoms, suggesting a possible benefit. However, these findings do not establish that chatbots cause improvement or that the results apply to all university students.”

3.3 Concise Language

Make every sentence contribute to the argument. Avoid repeating an idea, using long introductory phrases or adding information that does not help answer the assessment question. Concision does not mean omitting necessary evidence, context or critical evaluation; it means presenting these efficiently.

Less appropriateImproved
“It is important to point out that one of the things that should be considered when thinking about activity trackers is the fact that they can be expensive for students to purchase.”“The purchase cost of activity trackers may limit their accessibility to students.”

Why is this better? The revision removes filler and repetition, making the relevant point directly and using fewer words.

Your Turn

Rewrite the following sentence to make it more concise without changing its meaning.

Example to improve: “It is important to highlight the fact that the use of fitness trackers is something that may potentially help students to increase the amount of physical activity that they undertake.”

Suggested improved version

“Fitness trackers may help students increase their physical activity.”

3.4 Using Jargon Appropriately

Jargon refers to specialised words and phrases used within a discipline. Technical terminology can improve precision, but too much jargon makes writing harder to follow. Your audience should be able to understand the argument without repeatedly looking up unfamiliar terms.

  • Technical terminology: Use specialised terms when they add precision, but explain unfamiliar concepts in accessible language. For example, write “the device measures heart rate using an optical sensor that detects changes in blood flow” rather than “the device uses photoplethysmography (PPG)” without further explanation.
  • Acronyms and abbreviations: Write out unfamiliar terms when first introduced, followed by the abbreviation in brackets. For example, write “randomised controlled trial (RCT)” rather than immediately using “RCT”.
  • Statistical terminology: Explain what statistical findings mean rather than relying on technical values alone. For example, write “participants in the intervention group had higher physical activity levels, with a moderate effect size (d = 0.50)” rather than simply “the intervention produced d = 0.50”.
  • Unnecessarily complex terminology: Prefer simpler words when they communicate the same meaning. For example, write “use” rather than “utilise”, “before” rather than “prior to”, and “help” rather than “facilitate”, where appropriate.
  • Discipline-specific concepts: Explain specialised concepts that readers may not be familiar with. For example, write “the technology incorporates behaviour change techniques, such as goal setting and self-monitoring” rather than simply “the technology incorporates BCTs”.
Less appropriateImproved
“The intervention may increase PA via BCT-based self-regulatory mechanisms and improve adherence.”“The tracker uses behaviour change techniques (BCTs), such as goal-setting and self-monitoring, that may help users regulate their physical activity.”

Introduce an abbreviation only when it is useful. For example, write “Health Technology Assessment (HTA)” on first use, then “HTA” thereafter. Avoid abbreviating a term that appears only once or twice.

Your Turn

Rewrite the following sentence so that the technical language is understandable to a reader who is unfamiliar with the abbreviations.

Example to improve: “The RCT found that the intervention increased MVPA through BCTs, although long-term adherence remained low.”

Suggested improved version

“The randomised controlled trial (RCT) found that the intervention increased moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) through behaviour change techniques (BCTs), although continued use remained low.”

3.5 Using Consistent Language

Once you have chosen a term to describe something, use that same term consistently throughout your report. Avoid switching between different words for the same concept simply to make the writing sound more varied. In scientific writing, consistency is often more important than variety.

  • Choose one term: For example, if you introduce the technology as “wearable activity trackers”, continue using “wearable activity trackers” rather than alternating with “fitness devices”, “smartwatches” or “wearables”.
  • Keep names and labels consistent: If you refer to your target group as “university students”, do not switch between “students”, “young people” and “young adults” when you mean the same group.
  • Change terms only when the meaning changes: Use a different term when you genuinely mean something different, and make that distinction clear.
InconsistentImproved
“Wearable activity trackers can support self-monitoring. These smartwatches may also encourage goal setting. Fitness devices could therefore improve engagement.”“Wearable activity trackers can support self-monitoring and encourage goal setting. These trackers could therefore improve engagement.”

Why is this better? The revised version uses the same term for the same technology, making the argument easier to follow and avoiding the impression that different devices are being discussed.

Your Turn

Rewrite the following sentences using consistent terminology for the same technology and population.

Example to improve: “Wearable activity trackers may help university students monitor their activity. These smartwatches could also support young adults in setting goals. Fitness devices may therefore improve engagement among students.”

Suggested improved version

“Wearable activity trackers may help university students monitor their activity and set goals. These trackers may therefore improve engagement among university students.”

3.6 How to Write Sentences

Academic sentences should be clear, direct and easy to follow. A sentence does not need to be complicated to sound professional.

  • Avoid overly long sentences: Do not combine several different points into one sentence. Split it into two when this makes the argument easier to follow.
  • Keep each sentence focused: Make one main point at a time rather than moving between unrelated ideas.
  • Use clear, straightforward language: Express ideas as simply as possible without losing their meaning. For example, write “the study examined whether fitness trackers increased physical activity” rather than “the study sought to investigate the potential influence of wearable fitness tracking technologies on physical activity behaviours”.
  • Avoid unnecessary repetition: Do not repeat the same idea in different words within a sentence.
Less appropriateImproved
“Trackers are increasingly common and their apps can display lots of metrics and they are used by students who may be inactive and the evidence is mixed because studies differ.”“Wearable trackers can display activity measures, including daily step counts. However, differences between study populations and methods make their effects on physical activity difficult to compare.”

Why is this better? The revised version separates two ideas, removes unnecessary wording and makes the point easier to follow.

Your Turn

Rewrite the following example to make it clearer. Consider splitting the long sentence and removing unnecessary wording.

Example to improve: “Activity trackers can show daily step counts and provide goals and reminders, and they have become popular among students, although some studies have short follow-up periods and use different activity measures, which makes it difficult to determine whether improvements last.”

Suggested improved version

“Activity trackers can display daily step counts and provide goals and reminders. However, short follow-up periods and differences in activity measures across studies make it difficult to determine whether improvements are sustained.”

3.7 How to Write Paragraphs

A paragraph should develop one clear point, not simply contain several sentences on the same broad topic. Each sentence should contribute to the paragraph's overall purpose.

A practical paragraph structure

1. Point

Open with the main idea or judgement that the paragraph will develop.

2. Evidence

Support the point with relevant evidence. Give sufficient information about the study or source for the reader to judge its relevance.

3. Explanation and evaluation

Interpret the evidence, acknowledging important limitations, uncertainty or contrasts between findings.

4. Implication

Explain why the point matters for the argument or decision, and where appropriate lead towards the next paragraph.

List-like paragraphCoherent paragraph
“Trackers measure steps. They show goals. Studies have investigated whether they affect activity. Some people stop using them.”

The sentences are related, but there is no developed argument.

“Long-term engagement is a key consideration when evaluating wearable trackers. Although step-count feedback may encourage initial use, users who stop wearing the device will no longer receive that feedback. Evidence on retention and sustained activity change is therefore necessary before concluding that short-term improvements will continue.”

One main point is developed and connected to the assessment.

3.8 How to Write Sections

A section is one of the main parts of your report that you have been asked to write, such as “Clinical Effectiveness” or “Safety”. A subsection is a smaller topic within a section that may benefit from its own heading. For most sections in this assessment, subsections will not be necessary. Use them only when they genuinely help organise the discussion.

Plan the section before writing it

Step 1: Introduce the section

Briefly introduce what the section will examine and why it is important to the assessment. Keep this opening focused rather than repeating the wider background.

Step 2: Arrange paragraphs logically

Group material by theme or argument – for example, main outcomes, durability of effects, and limitations – rather than automatically giving every study its own paragraph.

Step 3: Summarise the section

End with a closing paragraph that concisely summarises the main ideas and what they mean for the assessment. Avoid introducing new evidence at this point.

If you use subsections, give them descriptive headings and keep the terminology consistent. Do not create a subsection for every paragraph or study. Avoid repeating background information across different HTA sections.

3.9 Knowing When to Break the Rules

The guidance in this chapter is a starting point, not a rigid set of rules. Following it closely can help you produce clear, professional writing, especially while you are developing your academic writing skills.

However, the most effective writing does not always follow every guideline. You may need a longer sentence to express a complex relationship, a technical term to communicate a precise idea, or a paragraph that does not follow the suggested structure.

The important thing is to understand why a guideline exists before choosing to depart from it. A deliberate exception should make your argument clearer or more accurate, not simply make the writing more complicated.

Remember: These are writing guidelines, not assessment rules. Always follow the requirements in your Assessment Brief, including the word limit, referencing and academic integrity requirements.

4.0 Learning Outcomes

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

  • Find relevant journal articles and evaluate their quality, timeliness and suitability for an HTA.
  • Identify appropriate sources of guidance, policy and legislation, and understand how their purposes differ.
  • Explain when news media may be relevant and why it cannot replace scientific or official sources.
  • Use Harvard Bath consistently for in-text citations and the reference list.

When conducting an HTA, you will need to draw on a range of sources, including journal articles, clinical guidance, policy documents and legislation. News articles may occasionally provide useful context when a technology has attracted public attention, but they should be used sparingly, if at all. The source you choose should match the point you are trying to make.

4.1 Journal Articles

Scientific journal articles provide the main evidence for evaluating whether a health technology works, how safe it is and how its effects compare with alternatives. Use them to support your assessment of clinical effectiveness, safety and other HTA domains, paying attention to the quality and relevance of individual studies.

Where to Find Journal Articles

There are several ways to find relevant scientific papers for your HTA:

  • Academic databases: Search PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, or the Cochrane Library for published research and evidence syntheses.
  • Search engines: Use Google Scholar to identify academic publications, or Google to locate articles and publisher websites. Check that you are reading a reliable, relevant source.
  • Individual journal websites: Browse or search journals directly for articles on your chosen technology and health problem. Some well-established, internationally recognised, peer-reviewed journals include BMJ, The Lancet, JAMA and npj Digital Medicine.
  • University of Bath Library: Use the Library website to discover academic literature and access articles available through the University.

Start with keywords describing your technology, target population and outcome. You can also check the reference lists of relevant articles to find further studies.

Evaluating the quality of scientific evidence

Different study designs answer different questions. The following table provides a simplified guide to common forms of evidence when assessing the effectiveness of a health technology.

Type of evidenceWhat it can tell you
Umbrella reviewsSummarise findings across multiple systematic reviews, offering a broad overview of a research area.
Systematic reviews and meta-analysesIdentify and critically synthesise studies on a defined question; meta-analysis statistically combines results where appropriate.
Randomised controlled trials (RCTs)Compare interventions using random allocation, helping assess whether a technology causes an outcome.
Cohort and case-control studiesExamine associations between exposures and outcomes, often in real-world settings.
Cross-sectional studiesDescribe characteristics or associations at one point in time, without establishing the direction of cause and effect.
Case reports and expert opinionHighlight individual experiences, emerging issues or professional perspectives, but provide limited evidence of effectiveness.

Note: The table is a guide, not a universal ranking. Evidence quality depends on how well the research was conducted and how directly it addresses your question. For example, interviews may be more useful than an RCT for understanding how people experience a technology. Assess each source's methods, sample, outcomes, limitations and relevance to your HTA.

How recent should your sources be?

Prioritise up-to-date studies, particularly for rapidly developing technologies such as artificial intelligence, wearable devices and digital health platforms. Evidence from more than a few years ago may examine versions of a technology that are no longer used. As a starting point, look for research published within approximately the last three to five years, then check whether newer evidence is available.

Older studies can still be valuable when they establish important concepts, provide landmark findings or remain directly relevant to the technology being assessed. Relevance and quality matter alongside publication date.

4.2 Guidance, Policy and Law

Guidelines, policy documents, legislation and organisational reports help you establish what is recommended, required or happening in practice. They are particularly useful when assessing the legal, organisational, ethical and patient-related implications of a health technology. Use them alongside scientific studies, not as substitutes for direct evidence of effectiveness.

Where to Find These Sources

Different sources serve different purposes:

  • Clinical guidelines: NICE and NHS England publish guidance and recommendations about healthcare practice and service delivery.
  • Government policy: The Department of Health and Social Care (DHSC) publishes health policies, strategies and plans.
  • Legislation: legislation.gov.uk is the official source for UK Acts and regulations. Check whether the legislation applies to your technology and the country or setting you are assessing.
  • Regulatory bodies: The MHRA provides guidance on medical devices and regulation, while the ICO provides information about data protection responsibilities.
  • Public health and statistics: The ONS and UKHSA provide population statistics, surveillance and public health information.
  • Third-sector organisations: Charities and independent organisations such as Healthwatch England and Mind publish reports, experiences and perspectives that can inform patient and social aspects of your HTA.
  • International organisations: The World Health Organization (WHO) provides international recommendations, health guidance and policy resources.

How to Use These Sources

Check the document's publication or update date, the relevant country and population, and whether it is law, guidance, policy or an organisational recommendation. These have different levels of authority. For third-sector publications, consider the underlying evidence and the organisation's perspective. Explain how each source informs your assessment rather than simply listing recommendations.

4.3 News Media

News media is not a substitute for peer-reviewed studies, official statistics, guidance or primary policy and legal documents. For most assignments, you will likely not need to cite news reporting at all.

When Might News Be Relevant?

An exception may be when a health technology becomes the subject of significant public attention and the reporting itself helps explain public concern, controversy or scrutiny. For example, Associated Press reported on a 2024 lawsuit following the death by suicide of a teenager who had interacted extensively with a Character.AI chatbot. Such reporting may provide context for discussing public concern about chatbot safety, but the claims in a lawsuit are not proof of causation or evidence of how frequently harm occurs.

Note: Use news sources sparingly, if at all. If you mention a reported incident, distinguish allegations from established findings and look for original research, regulatory statements or official documents to support your assessment. Do not use a news article in place of scientific evidence about effectiveness or safety.

Where to Find News

  • News search: Google News can help locate reporting on a particular technology or incident. Check the original article, its publication date and its sources. Google Trends can also help explore changes in public search interest, although this does not measure actual use or effectiveness.
  • Established news organisations: Search the websites of BBC News, Reuters, Associated Press, The Guardian and The Economist for coverage of relevant events.

If you cite a news report, make clear why it is relevant to your HTA and use it only to support claims about the reporting or public response, not to establish clinical effectiveness or causality.

4.4 Harvard Bath Referencing

Referencing allows readers to trace the evidence supporting your claims and distinguishes your own analysis from the ideas and findings of others. Consistent referencing also helps make your report clear, professional and academically credible.

5.0 Learning Outcomes

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

  • Distinguish descriptive writing from critical evaluation.
  • Assess the quality, applicability and limitations of individual studies and the wider body of research.
  • Select the highest-quality relevant evidence available and critically evaluate its strengths and remaining limitations.
  • Explain the implications of findings, strengths and limitations for an HTA decision.
  • Synthesise evidence across sources to develop a balanced, justified interpretation.

5.1 What Is Critical Evaluation?

Critical evaluation means judging how convincing and relevant the available evidence is, not simply describing what an author, study or report says. In an HTA, you need to explain both what is known about a technology and what the evidence allows you to conclude about its use in a particular population and setting.

A descriptive statement reports a finding. A critical statement interprets that finding, considers its limitations and explains what those limitations mean for a decision.

Descriptive writingCritical evaluation
“Smith et al. (2025) found that young adults using an AI chatbot experienced significant reductions in depressive symptoms after six weeks.”“Although Smith et al. (2025) reported significant reductions in depressive symptoms following six weeks of chatbot use, the absence of a control group makes it difficult to attribute these improvements to the intervention rather than natural symptom fluctuations or other influences. Consequently, the findings provide preliminary evidence of potential benefit but cannot establish that the chatbot is clinically effective.”

Criticality does not mean being negative. It means making a fair, justified judgement about the strengths, limitations and implications of evidence.

5.2 Critically Evaluating Evidence

Critical evaluation involves examining both the quality of individual studies and what the wider body of research tells us. These are related but distinct tasks.

Evaluating Individual Studies

When deciding how much weight to place on a particular study, consider the questions most relevant to the claim you are making:

  • Study design: Can the methods answer the question being asked? For example, can the design support a causal claim, or does it only show an association?
  • Population and setting: Do the participants, services and conditions reflect the group for whom the technology is being assessed?
  • Comparator: Was the technology compared with usual care, an alternative intervention or no intervention? Is that comparison relevant to your HTA?
  • Outcomes: Were important benefits, harms or experiences measured appropriately, and are the observed differences meaningful?
  • Precision and uncertainty: How large is the study, how precise are its estimates and what remains unknown?
  • Follow-up: Is the period of observation long enough to assess lasting benefits or delayed harms?
  • Bias and conflicts of interest: Could recruitment, missing data, reporting choices or funding relationships influence the findings?

Evaluating the Wider Body of Research

A single study rarely tells the whole story. Consider the research area as a whole to judge how confident you can be in the overall evidence:

  • Quantity and quality: How many relevant studies are available, and how robust are their methods? A large number of weak studies does not necessarily provide convincing evidence.
  • Consistency: Do studies reach similar conclusions, or are findings mixed? Can differences in populations, technologies, comparators or methods explain any disagreement?
  • Coverage and relevance: Has the evidence examined the populations, settings and outcomes that matter for your HTA, or are important groups and questions missing?
  • Publication and reporting bias: Could the available literature over-represent positive or statistically significant findings?
  • Currency: Is the evidence recent enough for the technology being assessed, particularly where products and clinical practice change quickly?
  • Evidence gaps: What remains uncertain, such as long-term outcomes, real-world use or effects in under-represented populations?

Bring these observations together into an overall judgement. Explain whether the evidence is convincing, mixed, preliminary or insufficient and why, rather than simply counting studies or listing their findings.

Note: Not every question applies equally to every source. A qualitative interview study might be especially useful for understanding acceptability, while a well-designed comparative trial may be more useful for estimating clinical effects. Assess the source against the purpose for which you are using it.

5.3 Advanced: Selecting and Critiquing High-Quality Evidence

It can be tempting to choose studies with obvious methodological weaknesses because they appear easier to critique. However, the purpose of an HTA is to evaluate the best available evidence, not to find evidence that is easiest to criticise. Prioritising weak studies may produce more limitations to discuss, but it can also lead to a less reliable assessment.

High-quality evidence still requires critical evaluation. A well-designed study may have limitations in its follow-up period, applicability, outcome measures or precision. Similarly, several rigorous studies may collectively leave uncertainty because they examine different populations, reach inconsistent conclusions or do not address long-term outcomes. Your task is to recognise both the strengths and the remaining limitations of individual studies and the wider body of research.

Less appropriate approachMore appropriate approach
"A small uncontrolled study reported improvements in depression scores following chatbot use, but its findings are unreliable because it lacked a control group (Smith et al., 2025).""A randomised controlled trial found that chatbot users experienced greater reductions in depressive symptoms than those receiving usual care (Jones et al., 2026). Although randomisation reduces the risk of confounding, the short follow-up period limits conclusions about whether these benefits are sustained."
"The randomised trial involved 500 participants and used validated depression measures, demonstrating that AI chatbots are effective (Patel et al., 2025).""The trial's large sample, randomised design and validated outcome measures increase confidence in its findings (Patel et al., 2025). However, the exclusion of participants with severe depression limits their applicability to young adults with more complex mental health needs."

Note: The best available evidence is not always high-quality evidence. Where research is limited, less robust studies may still be relevant, provided you explain their limitations and give their findings appropriate weight. Do not overlook useful evidence solely because a particular study design is unavailable.

5.4 Advanced: Drawing Out Implications

Evaluating evidence is only part of critical writing. The next step is to explain why your evaluation matters for the technology, target population or decision being considered. This is the difference between identifying a limitation and drawing out its implications.

Ask yourself: So what? If a study has a short follow-up period, what does that mean for confidence in lasting benefits? If participants differ from your intended population, how does that affect the relevance of its findings? If harms were not monitored systematically, what can decision-makers safely conclude?

Identifying a limitationDrawing out its implication
"The trial found that AI chatbot users experienced reduced depressive symptoms, although participants were only followed up for four weeks (Smith et al., 2025).""Although the trial demonstrated improvements in depressive symptoms after four weeks, the absence of longer-term follow-up means it is unclear whether these benefits persist (Smith et al., 2025). This limits the evidence supporting chatbot use as an ongoing intervention rather than a short-term source of support."
"The study recruited 180 students from a single UK university, limiting the generalisability of its findings (Jones et al., 2026).""Recruitment from a single university limits the applicability of the findings to the wider population of young adults, particularly those outside higher education (Jones et al., 2026). As the proposed technology is intended for broader community use, further evaluation across more diverse settings is needed before its benefits can be confidently generalised."

Try to complete the chain: finding → limitation or strength → implication → relevance to the HTA. An implication should be proportionate to the evidence: it might justify cautious use, additional safeguards, further research or greater confidence in a particular conclusion.

5.5 Advanced: Critical Synthesis

Critical synthesis brings findings from multiple sources together to develop an overall interpretation. Instead of summarising studies one by one, organise your discussion around the issue being assessed and explain where the evidence agrees, differs or leaves uncertainty.

When synthesising evidence, consider:

  • Agreement: Do different studies point towards a similar conclusion, and how convincing are their methods?
  • Disagreement: Could contrasting results be explained by differences in populations, versions of the technology, comparators, outcome measures or follow-up?
  • Relative weight: Which sources are most informative for the specific question, and why? Do not treat all studies as equally persuasive.
  • Overall interpretation: What can be concluded across the evidence, and where does uncertainty remain?
Study-by-study summaryCritical synthesis
"Smith et al. (2025) found that AI chatbots significantly reduced depressive symptoms compared with a waiting-list control. Jones et al. (2026) found no significant difference between chatbot use and a structured digital self-help programme. Patel et al. (2025) reported high levels of user satisfaction.""Evidence for the effectiveness of AI chatbots in reducing depressive symptoms remains mixed. Although one randomised trial reported benefits relative to a waiting-list control (Smith et al., 2025), another found no advantage over an established digital self-help programme (Jones et al., 2026). This difference may reflect the choice of comparator, suggesting that chatbots could offer benefits over receiving no immediate intervention without necessarily outperforming existing digital support. High user satisfaction (Patel et al., 2025) provides evidence of acceptability but does not resolve uncertainty about comparative clinical effectiveness."

Effective synthesis does not require every source to agree. It requires you to explain the pattern of evidence, assess the reasons for differences and make a justified judgement relevant to the HTA.

6.1 CUR: Domain Overview

The Health Problem and Current Use (CUR) domain establishes the context for assessing a health technology. It considers the target condition or health-related behaviour, the people affected, its scale and consequences, existing approaches to managing it, and the availability and patterns of use of the technology.

This domain is important because decision-makers need to understand what problem the technology is intended to address, for whom, and how it relates to current practice. A technology may be suitable for one group or setting but not another. Defining the population and existing approaches also provides the context for comparing technologies later in the HTA.

6.2 CUR: What To Cover

Organise the section around the aspects most relevant to your chosen health problem and technology. The four areas below are a guide to the content, not four compulsory subsections in your report.

Target Population

Clearly define the people for whom the technology is intended and establish how many are affected. Use population-specific evidence wherever possible.

Key questions

  • Who is the intended population?
  • How many people in this population are affected?
  • Do characteristics such as age, setting or other relevant factors change the nature or scale of the problem?

Target Condition

Describe the condition or health-related behaviour and why it matters. Depending on your topic, relevant information may include underlying mechanisms, natural history or course of disease, symptoms, risk factors, screening or diagnosis, prognosis, incidence, prevalence and the burden on individuals and society. Not every item is applicable to every technology or health problem.

Key questions

  • What is the condition or health problem, and how is it defined?
  • What are its risk factors, symptoms, natural course and prognosis, where relevant?
  • How common is it (for example, incidence or prevalence)?
  • What burden does it place on patients or individuals, services and society?
  • What screening or diagnostic methods are used, where relevant?

Current Management

Summarise how the problem is currently prevented, identified or managed, including relevant treatments, services or alternative technologies. Consider what these approaches offer and any gaps the proposed technology might address.

Key questions

  • What treatments, services or other approaches are currently available?
  • What are the principal alternatives to the proposed technology?
  • Are there limitations or unmet needs in current provision?

Utilisation

Describe the technology's current availability and patterns of use in practice. Where evidence exists, distinguish access, uptake and continued use, and consider whether these patterns differ across populations or settings.

Key questions

  • Is the technology currently available, and in what settings?
  • How many people use it in the real world?
  • What is known about uptake, frequency of use or sustained use?
  • Does use differ between relevant populations or settings?

Bringing the Section Together

Conclude the section with a brief paragraph that summarises the health problem, the limitations of current approaches and the potential need for the proposed technology.

Your closing paragraph should establish why the technology warrants further assessment, without making premature claims about its effectiveness, safety or overall suitability. These questions will be examined in subsequent HTA domains.

Key questions

  • What is the main problem affecting your target population?
  • What limitations or unmet needs exist in current approaches?
  • Why might the proposed technology be relevant to addressing these needs?

Note: These questions are intended to guide your understanding of what the CUR domain may cover, rather than provide a compulsory checklist. Depending on the purpose of your HTA, your chosen population and technology, and the available evidence, you may not need to address every question. Focus on the information most relevant to your assessment.

6.3 CUR: Sources You Should Draw From

Use evidence that establishes the scale and consequences of the health problem, describes current approaches to managing it, and shows how the technology is currently used. The most appropriate sources will depend on your population, setting and technology.

  • Journal articles: Use population-based surveys, epidemiological studies and reviews to describe prevalence, risk factors, prognosis and the burden of the condition.
  • Official statistics and public health reports: Use sources such as the Office for National Statistics (ONS) and NHS England data and information to establish the scale of the problem in a relevant population or setting.
  • Clinical guidelines and service information: Consult NICE guidance and relevant NHS or professional guidance to explain current recommended management and available alternatives.
  • Technology-use evidence: Use published surveys, implementation studies and credible service reports to describe availability, uptake and patterns of real-world use. Distinguish evidence of access from evidence of regular or sustained use.

Note: Prioritise evidence that relates directly to your chosen population and context. A figure for the general population may not accurately represent university students, for example. Check the date and definition of every statistic before including it.

This is especially relevant for rapidly developing technologies: even relatively small changes in their features may alter the benefits that can reasonably be expected. A clear technical description establishes exactly what is being assessed in the rest of the HTA.

8.1 EFF: Domain Overview

The Clinical Effectiveness (EFF) domain examines the health benefits and harms attributable to a technology, including their magnitude and overall net benefit (benefits weighed against harms). It asks whether the technology improves outcomes that matter to the people using it and how it compares with relevant alternatives.

The assessment distinguishes between two related concepts:

  • Efficacy: Whether a technology produces more benefit than harm under controlled or ideal circumstances, such as in an explanatory randomised controlled trial.
  • Effectiveness: Whether it produces more benefit than harm under usual healthcare conditions, such as in a pragmatic trial or real-world evaluation.

Why is this important? Whether a technology works is a fundamental consideration for patients, practitioners and decision-makers. Even a modest benefit may be worthwhile when compared with existing alternatives, but an intervention with no demonstrated benefit may be difficult to justify. Interpreting effectiveness requires attention to the size, duration and certainty of the effects, not simply whether a study reports a positive finding.

8.2 EFF: What To Cover

Focus on the clinical outcomes most relevant to your chosen technology, population and intended use. The four areas below reflect the EFF domain and are guidance rather than compulsory subsections for your report. Compare benefits with an appropriate alternative wherever possible.

Mortality and Morbidity

Consider whether the technology changes illness, symptoms or progression of a condition. Mortality outcomes may be important for some technologies, while changes in symptom severity or frequency will be more relevant for others.

Key questions

  • Does the technology prevent premature death, where this is a relevant outcome?
  • Does it change the severity or frequency of symptoms or other health problems?
  • Does it influence disease progression or the likelihood of future illness?
  • How do any observed benefits compare with usual care or another appropriate intervention?

Function

Examine how the technology influences everyday functioning, rather than assessing symptom change alone. Relevant outcomes might include bodily functioning, work, education, living circumstances or activities of daily living.

Key questions

  • Does the technology affect bodily functions or the ability to perform daily activities?
  • Does it influence work, study, independence or other aspects of everyday life?
  • Are any improvements sustained beyond the immediate intervention period?

Quality of Life

Consider whether the technology improves how people experience their health and daily lives. Evidence may use general, health-related or condition-specific quality-of-life measures.

Key questions

  • Does the technology improve general, health-related or condition-specific quality of life?
  • Were the outcomes measured using appropriate and validated instruments?
  • How meaningful and consistent are the reported improvements?

Accuracy

When a technology measures, screens for, diagnoses or monitors a condition, consider whether its results are reliable and valid. This may be particularly important where decisions or treatment recommendations depend on the measurement.

Key questions

  • How reliable and valid is the technology's measurement or assessment?
  • How does its accuracy compare with an appropriate reference standard or existing method?
  • Could inaccurate results affect decisions about care or the interpretation of effectiveness?

Note: Not all four areas will be relevant to every technology. Your analysis should also consider study design, comparator, sample, follow-up, effect magnitude and the limitations of the evidence. An efficacy result obtained under ideal conditions does not automatically establish effectiveness in routine practice.

Bringing the Section Together

Conclude with a concise judgement about the overall pattern and magnitude of benefits, how these compare with alternative approaches, and how confidently they can be applied to your chosen population and setting. Consider any important uncertainty or trade-off between benefits and harms.

Key questions

  • What is the overall evidence that the technology improves outcomes relevant to your population?
  • Are the benefits meaningful and sustained, and how do they compare with alternatives?
  • How confident can you be in the findings, given the design and limitations of the available studies?

Note: These questions illustrate the issues that the EFF domain may cover, rather than provide a compulsory checklist. Address the outcomes and questions most relevant to your HTA.

8.3 EFF: Sources You Should Draw From

Choose sources that help you address the clinical effectiveness of your chosen technology. Select evidence appropriate to the question, context and available research.

  • Systematic reviews and meta-analyses: Use relevant syntheses to identify the overall direction, consistency and limitations of evidence.
  • Randomised and comparative trials: Prioritise well-conducted studies with appropriate comparators, validated outcomes and suitable follow-up.
  • Pragmatic and real-world studies: Use routine-practice evaluations to consider whether benefits generalise beyond controlled trials.
  • Validation and outcomes research: For diagnostic or monitoring functions, consult studies reporting reliability, validity, accuracy or patient-relevant outcomes.

Note: A statistically significant result does not automatically demonstrate a meaningful or lasting benefit. Interpret effects in relation to the comparator, outcome measure, follow-up and study limitations.

9.1 SAF: Domain Overview

The Safety (SAF) domain examines unwanted or harmful effects caused by, or potentially associated with, using a health technology. Depending on the technology, harms may be physical, psychological or related to other aspects of health and wellbeing.

Safety information is essential for developing a balanced assessment of a technology's overall diagnostic or therapeutic value and for informing patients, practitioners and decision-makers. A technology may offer clinical benefits but still present important risks, particularly for certain groups or patterns of use.

Reliable evidence about harms can be difficult to collect, especially when adverse events are rare, inconsistently reported or emerge only after prolonged use. Assess both what is known about safety and what remains uncertain.

9.2 SAF: What To Cover

Focus on the potential unwanted effects of the technology and their implications for the intended population. The four areas below follow the Seminar's key questions and are guides to coverage, not compulsory subsections in your report.

Patient Safety

Identify the types, frequency and severity of harm that may be associated with the technology. Consider how these risks compare with alternatives, whether they vary with exposure or time, and whether particular groups may be more susceptible. Where relevant, describe safeguards for identifying and responding to harm.

Key questions

  • How safe is the technology in comparison with relevant alternatives?
  • What unwanted or harmful effects have been observed or reported?
  • Do harms depend on the frequency, intensity or duration of use?
  • Do risks change over time, or are some population groups more susceptible?

Function

Consider whether unwanted effects interfere with bodily functions, work, living conditions or the ability to perform activities of daily living. Distinguish the consequences of harm from the intended functional benefits assessed in Clinical Effectiveness.

Key questions

  • Could use of the technology adversely affect bodily or psychological functioning?
  • Could harmful effects interfere with work, study, living conditions or everyday activities?

Quality of Life

Examine whether unwanted effects influence general, health-related or condition-specific quality of life. Focus on harms relevant to the users and setting rather than repeating an assessment of clinical benefits.

Key questions

  • Could the technology adversely affect general or health-related quality of life?
  • Are any harmful effects particularly important for people with the condition being assessed?

Accuracy

Where the technology measures, monitors, screens or identifies a condition or risk, consider the reliability and validity of its outputs and the consequences of inaccurate results. This may include inappropriate reassurance, missed risks or unnecessary interventions.

Key questions

  • How reliable and valid is the relevant test, tool or output?
  • Could false, inaccurate or inconsistent results create safety risks?

Bringing the Section Together

Summarise the most important harms, which users may be most vulnerable, and any remaining uncertainties. Where appropriate, briefly identify the safeguards or further evidence needed to use the technology safely.

Key questions

  • What are the principal safety concerns, and how do they compare with alternatives?
  • Which safety issues remain uncertain, and what safeguards may be required?

Note: These questions illustrate what the SAF domain may cover; they are not a compulsory checklist. Address the issues most relevant to your technology and population. A lack of reported adverse events does not necessarily mean that the technology is safe.

9.3 SAF: Sources You Should Draw From

Choose sources that help you address the safety of your chosen technology. Select evidence appropriate to the question, context and available research.

  • Trials and observational safety studies: Look for prespecified adverse-event reporting, withdrawals, long-term follow-up and subgroup findings.
  • Post-implementation monitoring: Use published incident reports, service audits and post-market evidence when available.
  • Regulatory safety information: Consult the MHRA for relevant medical-device alerts and guidance.
  • Patient and qualitative evidence: Consider credible accounts of distress, usability failures or unintended consequences, while distinguishing reports from established causal evidence.

Note: Do not confuse a lack of reported harms with evidence that there are no harms. Assess whether studies were designed, powered and conducted long enough to detect relevant safety outcomes.

10.1 ECO: Domain Overview

The Costs and Economic Effectiveness (ECO) domain assesses the resources a health technology requires and whether its benefits justify its costs compared with relevant alternatives. In publicly funded systems, resources are limited, so choosing one intervention can affect what else can be provided.

The aim is to inform value-for-money decisions, not simply to identify the cheapest option. Economic evaluation brings together information about costs, health outcomes and resource use. A full economic model can be complex, and published analyses require careful interpretation.

10.2 ECO: What To Cover

Focus on the aspects most relevant to your chosen technology, population and setting. The topics below are a guide to the content, not a required set of subsections in your report.

Resource Use and Direct Costs

Identify what is required to introduce, provide and maintain the technology. Consider how these requirements differ from those of the comparator.

Key questions

  • What staff time, equipment, licences, premises or training are needed?
  • What are the measured or estimated costs of providing the technology and its alternatives?
  • What ongoing maintenance, monitoring or replacement costs may arise?

Comparative Economic Value

Assess whether additional expenditure produces worthwhile additional health benefits. Distinguish clinical outcomes from costs and examine the perspective taken in the evaluation.

Key questions

  • Which health outcomes are valued in the analysis?
  • How do the costs and outcomes compare with the relevant alternative?
  • Does the study use a suitable economic evaluation approach?

Budget Impact and Uncertainty

Consider the implications of wider adoption and whether assumptions are likely to hold across groups, settings or time periods.

Key questions

  • How many people might receive the technology, and what would this mean for the overall budget?
  • Do costs vary by population, provider, region or level of uptake?
  • Have important costs, outcomes and uncertainties been omitted?

Common Types of Economic Analysis

Different methods answer different questions. You do not need to use every method in your report; identify the approach used by the evidence you discuss.

ApproachWhat it examines
Cost-of-illness analysisThe economic burden of a health problem, rather than the comparative value of an intervention.
Cost-minimisation analysisThe less costly option when equivalent outcomes can reasonably be assumed.
Cost-effectiveness analysisDifferences in costs relative to differences in health outcomes.
Cost-utility analysisCosts relative to outcomes such as quality-adjusted life years (QALYs).
Cost-consequence analysisCosts and different outcomes presented separately, without combining them into one measure.
Cost-benefit analysisCosts and benefits expressed in monetary terms.
Budget-impact analysisThe likely effect of adoption on a particular organisation’s or health system’s budget.

Bringing the Section Together

End by explaining what can reasonably be concluded about value for money from the available evidence, including important cost categories and uncertainties. Do not infer cost-effectiveness from a low purchase price alone.

Key questions

  • What are the most important drivers of cost?
  • What do comparative economic findings suggest, if available?
  • What uncertainties would affect a decision to adopt the technology?

Note: These questions indicate the kinds of issues the ECO domain may cover; they are not a compulsory checklist. You may not need to address every question. Prioritise what is relevant to your assessment and supported by the available evidence.

10.3 ECO: Sources You Should Draw From

Choose sources that help you address the cost and economic effectiveness of your chosen technology. Select evidence appropriate to the question, context and available research.

  • Published economic evaluations: Use cost-effectiveness, cost-utility and other comparative economic studies, checking their perspective, assumptions and outcome measures.
  • Health technology assessment reports: Consult NICE guidance and assessments where relevant to pricing, value and decision-making.
  • Service and implementation evidence: Look for staffing, licensing, equipment and support requirements reported by actual services.
  • Cost and budget information: Use transparent official tariffs, published price schedules and service budgets where available, making the date and setting clear.

Note: If no directly relevant economic evaluation exists, state this clearly. You may still describe likely resource requirements and cost drivers without presenting an unsupported claim of cost-effectiveness.

11.1 ETH: Domain Overview

The Ethical Analysis (ETH) domain considers the values, principles and moral questions raised by introducing or not introducing a health technology. It moves beyond asking whether a technology is technically possible or clinically beneficial to consider whether its use is acceptable and fair.

Ethical issues matter because technologies can affect the dignity, autonomy, wellbeing and interests of patients, families, professionals and society. Assessment should recognise competing values and potential unintended consequences, rather than assuming that benefits for one group justify every impact on others.

11.2 ETH: What To Cover

Focus on the aspects most relevant to your chosen technology, population and setting. The topics below are a guide to the content, not a required set of subsections in your report.

Benefit–Harm Balance

Consider who experiences potential benefits and harms, including patients, carers, other service users and the wider community. Some impacts may be indirect or unintended.

Key questions

  • Who may benefit, and who might experience harm?
  • Could implementation or non-implementation have unexpected consequences?
  • Are there ethical constraints on how the technology can be researched or evaluated?

Autonomy and Choice

Assess whether people can make meaningful, informed choices about using the technology, particularly where they may be vulnerable or dependent on services.

Key questions

  • Can users give informed agreement and decline or discontinue use?
  • Could the technology encourage dependence or influence decisions inappropriately?
  • Are additional safeguards needed for vulnerable populations?

Respect, Dignity and Privacy

Examine whether the technology respects users’ values, privacy and cultural or personal circumstances. Distinguish ethical privacy concerns from specific legal duties.

Key questions

  • Could use compromise dignity, confidentiality or the sense of being respected?
  • Is the technology appropriate for users with different cultural or personal values?
  • How transparent is the technology about its capabilities and limitations?

Justice and Equity

Consider fairness in access and the distribution of opportunities, risks and resources across groups.

Key questions

  • Could some groups be excluded or disadvantaged?
  • Would implementation shift limited resources away from other people or services?
  • Are the burdens and benefits fairly distributed?

Bringing the Section Together

Bring the ethical arguments together by explaining which values are most important in the intended setting and what safeguards or conditions might address competing concerns. Ethical judgements should be justified, not merely listed.

Key questions

  • Which ethical tensions most affect the proposed use?
  • Whose perspectives or interests may be overlooked?
  • What conditions might make implementation more ethically acceptable?

Note: These questions indicate the kinds of issues the ETH domain may cover; they are not a compulsory checklist. You may not need to address every question. Prioritise what is relevant to your assessment and supported by the available evidence.

11.3 ETH: Sources You Should Draw From

Choose sources that help you address the ethical analysis of your chosen technology. Select evidence appropriate to the question, context and available research.

  • Peer-reviewed ethics and empirical research: Use relevant ethical analyses and qualitative work on autonomy, privacy, fairness or experiences of technology.
  • Patient and stakeholder perspectives: Draw on interviews, surveys and engagement reports to understand whose values and interests are affected.
  • Professional and ethical guidance: Consult authoritative guidance on consent, confidentiality and appropriate practice where relevant.
  • Equality and public-interest reports: Use credible evidence about exclusions, inequitable access and potential distributional consequences.

Note: Do not treat an ethical issue as settled simply because a legal requirement has been met. Ethical concerns often extend beyond minimum compliance.

  • Summarise the most important findings across the nine HTA domains.
  • Reach a justified conclusion about whether a technology should be used for a particular health problem and population.
  • Develop concrete recommendations that follow from the evidence and acknowledge remaining uncertainty.

15.1 Bringing Together the HTA Findings

Your conclusion should begin with a brief overview of the main findings across the nine HTA domains. This is not an opportunity to repeat every result. Instead, draw together the findings that matter most for deciding whether the technology should be used.

Refer back to the nine domains introduced in Chapter 2: An Overview of HTA as you bring your findings together.

Aim to summarise the key finding from each domain in anything from part of a sentence to one or two sentences, depending on how much that domain contributes to your overall conclusion. Focus on the implications of the findings, rather than repeating the detailed analysis from earlier sections.

Keep this overview proportionate. Give greater emphasis to the domains that most influence your overall judgement, while showing that you have considered the wider implications of the technology.

15.2 Answering the Central HTA Question

As discussed in Section 2.1, the overarching question is:

Should this technology be used for this health problem and population, and, if so, under what conditions?

Your conclusion needs to answer this question directly, drawing on the findings you have just brought together. Explain whether the evidence supports adopting the technology, using it only in defined circumstances, evaluating it further before wider use, or not recommending it at present.

A convincing conclusion does not simply count the advantages and disadvantages. It weighs their importance, explains what remains uncertain and makes clear why the overall balance supports your decision. A conditional judgement is often appropriate, but it should still be a clear judgement.

15.3 Developing Concrete Recommendations

Once you have reached a conclusion, explain what should happen in practice. Recommendations should follow logically from your assessment rather than introduce unrelated ideas or end with a vague call for further research.

Depending on your findings, consider specifying:

  • Action: Should the technology be adopted, piloted, restricted to certain circumstances, or not introduced?
  • Population and setting: Who is it suitable for, and where should it be used?
  • Conditions and safeguards: What training, oversight, accessibility measures or safety procedures are needed?
  • Implementation: What staffing, funding and organisational arrangements would be required?
  • Monitoring and further evidence: Which outcomes, risks and costs should be measured, and what evidence would justify changing the recommendation?

For example, “Further research is needed” is not sufficiently specific. A more actionable recommendation would identify the key uncertainty, the population that needs to be studied and the outcomes or follow-up period needed to resolve it.

Worked Example: Conclusion and Recommendations

Topic: AI Chatbots for Mental Health in Young Adult Populations

AI-based mental health chatbots offer a promising and accessible approach to addressing unmet mental health needs among young adults. Evidence from randomised controlled trials and systematic reviews suggests that these technologies can produce meaningful short-term reductions in depressive and anxiety symptoms, particularly among individuals experiencing mild to moderate difficulties (Smith et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026). Their accessibility, flexibility and relatively low delivery costs further support their potential integration into existing mental health services. However, uncertainties remain regarding long-term effectiveness, responses to psychological distress and cost-effectiveness compared with established interventions (Williams et al., 2026). Overall, the available evidence supports adopting AI chatbots as a supplementary form of mental health support, provided that appropriate safeguards and professional support remain available.

A phased introduction within university and community wellbeing services is recommended, initially targeting young adults aged 18–25 experiencing mild to moderate symptoms of depression or anxiety. Chatbots should be offered as an optional supplement to existing services, with an initial assessment to identify individuals requiring more intensive support. Providers should implement clear crisis escalation procedures, including access to human assistance, and require staff training in identifying and responding to psychological risk. Services should also provide transparent information about data collection and offer non-digital alternatives to ensure accessibility. During the first 12 months, providers should monitor depressive and anxiety symptoms using validated measures, alongside engagement, adverse events and implementation costs. Findings should be reviewed at six and twelve months to inform adjustments to service delivery and decisions about wider implementation.
